# **Overview of Key Activities & Accomplishments**

Through the CIP Fellow program, I accomplished three main things, solidification of foundational cyber-infrastructure knowledge, exposure to academic and research HPC communities and resources, and application to general sysadmin and SDSC RDS HPC projects.

As a systems engineer and admin without formal or proper training, my first focus was to understand the basics and was able to do so successfully. I credit mainly the SDSC COMPLECs series and briefly used the Cornell Virtual Workshops and HPRC ACES containers series. This covered general Linux, data transfers, interactive computing, batch computing, HPC hardware, containerization, and ML/DL.

I also attended many larger trainings, specifically NVIDIA GTC, AI for Science Bootcamp, Red Hat Summit, AnsibleFest, Nasa end-to-end LLM Bootcamp, SDSC CIML Summer Institute, PEARC24, and the SDSC HPC-DS Summer Institute. Not only did these opportunities further strengthen my knowledge, they displayed different compute resources, infrastructure, solutions and tools. Many of which I took inspiration from and have plans to further explore and implement. Through these training sessions, I also interacted and conversed with many individuals across various communities and institutions. I engaged in MATCH and NAIRR, by advising researchers from other institutions on Expanse usage, facilitating a NAIRR PEARC Hackathon and contributing to the upcoming of NAIRR and its organization and structure. I feel more connected and intertwined with ACCESS, NAIRR, MATCH, even SDSC communities and the CIP-Fellow cohort. 

Part of my job with the SDSC RDS division is supporting the Custom Compute Clusters. Prior to the CIP program, I was only able to provide basic levels of user support, with account creation and password resets. Through the program, I was able to better facilitate and assist users with running jobs, having issues with their environment and much more. I also have new ideas to improve the RDS clusters, like implementing gayleo and better user monitoring resources as well as adding more detailed user documentation. In general, the overall knowledge and exposure has expanded my perspective on cyberinfrastructure and I’m excited to find new innovations and use cases. 

# **Challenges**

Some of the challenges I encountered were identifying a few topics I wanted to focus on rather than pursuing all interests, finding additional training on my own, and balancing my time with my other projects/teams. 

Cyberinfrastructure is a very broad field that encompasses hundreds of other subtopics. While I appreciate the freedom to find which topic(s) to pursue, my interest list had grown vastly before I realized how inefficient it was. And I struggled to narrow it down, which took me unfortunately a bit of time to realize and optimize. From there, it was difficult to research the available trainings and find efficient and effective ones. 

I also found some trainings a bit hard to understand and tangibilize as a non-researcher with no immediate compute need or use. I felt this often with the ML/DL topics, but in the end, found some benefit as a general way to practice and code. 

Another challenge was balancing time. The time committed was not as efficient as I would have liked it to be. Figuring out what topics to pursue and then finding actual effective trainings took a lot of time which could have been used for more practice. Also organizing and juggling 50% of my work time to a fellowship program was more difficult than expected especially when the other 50% had demanding and/or urgent deadlines or last minute requests.
